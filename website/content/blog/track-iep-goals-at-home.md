An IEP can feel like a document that lives at school. It is written in meetings, kept in a file and reviewed a few times a year. But you see your child every day. If you learn how to track your child's IEP goals at home, you bring something the school does not have: a steady view of how your child is doing outside the classroom.

This does not need to be a big project. A few lines a week, kept in one place, is enough to make the next review meeting clearer and calmer.

## What is an IEP, and who writes one?

An IEP (individualised education plan) sets out specific goals for a child who needs extra support at school, along with how the school will help. In India, some inclusive schools write IEPs, often with input from a special educator. Many families also have separate goal sheets from a therapy centre for speech, occupational or behavioural therapy. In the UAE, schools commonly write IEPs for students of determination.

The names and formats vary. What matters for you is the list of goals. Each goal usually describes a skill, the conditions it is practised in, and how the school will know it has been met.

## Why track IEP goals at home at all?

School reports tend to arrive once a term. A lot happens between them. Tracking at home helps you:

- Notice small changes as they happen, instead of trying to remember them months later.
- See whether a skill used at school also shows up at home, with family or in new places.
- Spot patterns, such as harder weeks after a holiday or easier mornings than evenings.
- Ask clearer questions at review meetings.
- Share useful details with therapists who may never see your child at school.

Home tracking is not about testing your child or checking up on the school. It is simply a record of what you see.

## Start by reading the goals closely

Before you track anything, sit with the IEP for ten minutes. For each goal, try to answer three questions:

1. **What is the skill?** For example, asking for help using words, or following a two-step instruction.
2. **What does success look like?** Some goals say "4 out of 5 times" or "with one prompt". Others are less specific.
3. **Could I see this at home?** Some goals are classroom-only, like copying from the board. Others, like greeting people or taking turns, show up at home all the time.

If a goal is unclear, write down your question. "What does 'with minimal support' look like in practice?" is a perfectly reasonable thing to ask your child's teacher or special educator.

## Choose two or three goals to watch

You do not need to track every goal. Most IEPs have more goals than a busy family can follow. Pick two or three that:

- Come up naturally at home.
- Matter to your child and your family day to day.
- You feel unsure about, and would like a clearer picture of.

You can change your choice each term. Tracking a few goals well is more useful than tracking all of them occasionally.

## How to record IEP progress at home

Keep it short. A note you actually write beats a perfect system you abandon. For each moment you want to record, jot down:

- **The date** and roughly what was happening (breakfast, park, car ride).
- **Which goal** it relates to.
- **What your child did**, in plain words.
- **How much help they needed**: none, a gentle reminder, a model to copy, or hands-on help.
- **How they seemed**: relaxed, tired, frustrated, proud.

Here is an example:

> 14 Nov, after school snack. Goal: asking for help with words. Couldn't open the juice box, said "help please" after I waited and looked at him. No other prompt. Seemed calm.

That took less than a minute to write, and it tells a teacher a lot.

### How often should I write notes?

Aim for a rhythm you can keep. For many parents, that is two or three notes a week, plus anything that surprises you. If a week goes by with nothing, that is fine. Pick it up again the next week.

### Where should I keep them?

Anywhere you will reliably find them again: a notebook kept by the kettle, a notes app, a shared family document or a dedicated app. The key is having **one place**, so notes from different weeks can be read together.

## Notice the level of help, not just success

Parents often record "did it" or "didn't do it". The more useful detail is usually how much help was needed. A child who needed hands-on help in June and only a reminder in October has made real progress, even if the goal is not yet met.

Help levels also make your notes easier to compare with school. Teachers and therapists often think in the same terms, so you will be speaking a shared language.

## IEP progress monitoring for parents: reading your notes

Once a month, read back through your notes. You are looking for patterns, not a score. Ask yourself:

- Is the level of help changing over time?
- Does the skill show up in more places or with more people?
- Are there times of day, settings or people that make it easier or harder?
- Has anything new appeared that is not in the IEP at all?

Write two or three sentences summarising what you see. This is the heart of IEP progress monitoring for parents: not charts, just honest observations gathered over time.

Try to compare your child only with their own earlier self. Every child moves at their own pace, and the goals were written for your child specifically. Our article on [developmental milestones without comparing](/blog/developmental-milestones-without-comparing) has more on this.

## Bringing your notes to school

Before a review meeting, turn your monthly summaries into a short page. Include:

- Each goal you watched, with one or two real examples from home.
- What has changed since the last meeting.
- What you have not seen at home, and any questions about it.
- Anything you would like the school to try or explain.

Share it with the teacher a few days before if you can. It gives everyone time to think, and it keeps the meeting focused on your child. Our guide to [preparing for an IEP meeting](/blog/preparing-for-an-iep-meeting) walks through the rest of the meeting in more detail.

## When home and school see different things

Sometimes your child does something easily at home that the school has not seen, or the other way round. This is common and not a sign that anyone is wrong. Settings, people, noise and routine all affect how a child shows a skill.

Treat differences as useful information. Describe what you see, ask what the school sees, and talk together about what might explain the gap. If you have concerns about your child's progress or wellbeing, raise them with the school, your child's therapist or your paediatrician.

## Keeping it light

Tracking should never turn home into a second classroom. Your child does not need to know you are watching for a goal, and you do not need to set up tests. Just notice, and write a line or two when something stands out.

## A short summary

- Read the goals and note anything unclear.
- Pick two or three goals that show up at home.
- Write short notes: what happened, how much help, how your child seemed.
- Read back monthly and look for patterns against your child's own history.
- Bring a one-page summary to school meetings.

*This article is general information, not medical or educational advice. Your child's teachers and therapists know your child best, so talk with them about what to watch for at home.*
