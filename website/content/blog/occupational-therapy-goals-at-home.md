Occupational therapy (OT) helps children build the skills they need for everyday life: getting dressed, eating, playing, writing, and managing how their body and senses respond to the world. Sessions might be once or twice a week. Home is where those skills are actually used every day.

That is why many parents ask about occupational therapy activities at home. The good news is that you usually don't need special equipment or extra time. The most useful practice often happens inside routines you already have, with your therapist's guidance.

## Start with your child's OT goals

Before you change anything at home, make sure you know what your child is working on. Ask your child's occupational therapist:

- Which goals are you focusing on right now?
- What does each goal look like in daily life?
- Which routines at home could support it?
- How much help should I give, and how should I step back over time?
- Is there anything I should avoid, for safety or comfort?

Write the answers down. If a goal is phrased in technical terms, ask what it means in plain words. "Bilateral coordination" might become "using both hands together, like holding a jar and opening the lid".

Home practice works best when it matches what happens in sessions. Doing different things at home can be confusing for your child, so agree the plan with the therapist first.

## Why routines work better than extra exercises

Daily routines repeat. Your child gets dressed every morning, eats several times a day and plays most afternoons. That repetition gives many natural chances to practise, without turning home into a therapy room.

Routines also have a real purpose. Putting on a shoe to go to the park means more to a child than a practice task. And it keeps family life feeling like family life.

## OT goals at home: everyday routines

Below are general ideas for how OT goals often show up in daily life. They are starting points for a conversation with your therapist, not a programme to follow. Your therapist will know which fit your child.

### Dressing

Dressing involves balance, coordination, planning steps and fine motor skills like buttons and zips.

- Give extra time on mornings that aren't rushed, such as weekends.
- Let your child finish the last step themselves, like pulling up a sock you've started. Many therapists suggest working backwards like this so the child gets the satisfaction of finishing.
- Lay clothes out in order, if your therapist suggests it.
- Ask whether loose clothes, larger buttons or easier fastenings would help for now.

### Mealtimes

Mealtimes can involve sitting, using utensils, trying new textures and coping with smells and sounds.

- Ask the therapist about seating. Feet supported and a table at a comfortable height can make a real difference.
- Let your child help with simple tasks: pouring from a small jug, spreading, tearing roti or chapati, passing dishes.
- If your child finds certain foods or textures hard, ask your therapist how to approach this. Avoid pressure; a calm table helps everyone.

If your child has any difficulty with chewing or swallowing, please follow the advice of your therapist or paediatrician before trying anything new at mealtimes.

### Play

Play is where much of OT happens, often without your child noticing.

- **Hands and fingers:** play dough, building blocks, threading large beads, tearing paper for a collage, peeling stickers.
- **Whole body:** playground time, crawling through cushion tunnels, carrying shopping bags, helping to water plants.
- **Both hands together:** opening containers, holding paper while cutting, pouring water in the bath.

Ask your therapist which kinds of play fit your child's goals, and which equipment is safe at their age and stage.

### Handwriting and fine motor skills

Handwriting builds on many earlier skills: posture, shoulder stability, hand strength and finger control. Practising letters alone isn't always the best route.

- Ask the therapist about posture, pencil grip and whether any aids are recommended.
- Try writing and drawing on different surfaces, such as a chalkboard on the wall or paper taped to a table, if suggested.
- Keep handwriting tasks short and finish while your child is still enjoying it.
- Use real reasons to write: a shopping list, a birthday card, a name on a drawing.

### Calm and regulation

Some OT goals relate to how a child manages sensory input and stays settled. These are very individual. One child seeks movement; another finds it overwhelming.

This is an area where your therapist's guidance is especially important. Ask what helps your child feel calm, what signs to look for, and what to do when things become too much.

## Keeping home practice safe and positive

A few principles apply to almost every family:

- **Agree it with your therapist.** Only use activities and equipment they have recommended for your child.
- **Keep it short and light.** A few minutes inside a routine is often enough.
- **Follow your child's cues.** If they are tired, upset or in pain, stop.
- **Supervise.** Especially with small objects, climbing, swings or anything around water.
- **Praise effort.** Trying matters, whatever the result.
- **Don't do everything.** It is fine to choose one routine this month and leave the rest.

There is no need to feel guilty about days when nothing happens. Family life is busy, and rest matters too.

## Notice and note what you see

Short notes help you and the therapist see how skills are carrying over to home. After a routine, jot down:

- Which goal it related to.
- What your child did.
- How much help they needed: none, a reminder, showing them, or hands-on help.
- How they seemed.

Bring these notes to sessions. They show the therapist whether skills are being used in real life, and help them adjust the plan. Our article on [what to write after a therapy session](/blog/what-to-write-after-a-therapy-session) has a simple template you can copy.

Over a few months, reading back these notes can show real change, such as needing less help with buttons, or managing a whole meal at the table. Compare your child only with their own earlier self. ## In short

- Ask your child's occupational therapist which goals to focus on and how they show up in daily life.
- Use routines you already have: dressing, mealtimes, play and writing.
- Keep practice short, safe and agreed with the therapist.
- Note what you see and share it at sessions.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your child's therapist knows your child best. Please agree any home activities with them and raise concerns with your therapist or paediatrician.*
