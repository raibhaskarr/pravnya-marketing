An IEP meeting can feel like a lot. There is a table of people, a document full of goals, and not much time. Many parents walk out thinking of the question they meant to ask.

A little preparation helps. You do not need to become an expert in education. You only need to bring what you already know best: your child.

This guide covers what to do before, during and after an IEP or school review meeting. It applies whether your child is at an inclusive school in India working with a special educator, or at a school in the UAE that writes an individual plan for students of determination.

## What is an IEP meeting, and who will be there?

An IEP (individualised education plan) is a written plan for how a school will support your child. Schools may call it something else: an individual learning plan, a support plan or a review. The idea is the same. It sets out goals for your child, and the support the school will give to help them get there.

Who attends depends on the school. You may meet:

- your child's class teacher
- a special educator, learning support teacher or inclusion coordinator
- a school counsellor or psychologist
- therapists who work with your child, such as a speech or occupational therapist
- a head of section or principal, for some meetings

In India, the setup varies a lot between schools. Some have a full learning support team. Others have one special educator who covers many classes. In the UAE, many schools have an inclusion team that leads the process. If you are unsure who will attend, it is fine to ask the school beforehand.

## How should I prepare in the weeks before?

### Ask for the current plan and any reports

If your child already has a plan, ask for a copy before the meeting. Read the goals slowly. For each one, ask yourself: do I understand what this means in everyday terms? Have I seen this skill at home?

If there are reports from teachers or therapists, ask for those too. It is much easier to think about them at your own table than to read them for the first time in the meeting.

### Write down what you see at home

You see your child in places the school never does: at breakfast, at the park, at a family function, in the car. That view is valuable.

In the weeks before the meeting, jot down short, specific notes. For example:

- "Asked for water using a full sentence, twice this week."
- "Got upset when the routine changed on Sunday. Calmed down after about ten minutes with his blanket."
- "Can now button her school shirt, but it takes a long time in the morning rush."

Dated, concrete notes like these are more useful than a general feeling. If you already keep notes after sessions, you are halfway there. Our guide on [what to write after a therapy session](/blog/what-to-write-after-a-therapy-session) has a simple format you can reuse.

### Decide on your two or three priorities

You cannot cover everything in one meeting. Before you go, pick two or three things that matter most to your family right now. It might be communication with classmates, getting through transitions, or independence with self-care.

Write them at the top of your page. If time runs short, you will still have raised what matters most.

## What should I bring to the meeting?

A short list is enough:

- a copy of the current plan, with your notes on it
- your home observations, ideally grouped by goal
- any recent reports from outside therapists or your child's paediatrician
- your list of priorities and questions
- a pen and notebook, or a way to take notes on your phone

You may also bring a family member or a trusted person for support. Many parents find it helps to have one person talk and the other take notes. If you would like to bring your child's private therapist, ask the school in advance.

## What questions should I ask at an IEP meeting?

Questions help turn a document into a shared plan. Here are some parents often find useful.

### About the goals

- What does this goal look like in practice? Can you give an example?
- How will we know when it has been met?
- Is this goal realistic for this term, given where my child is now?
- Which goals do you think matter most right now?

### About support and progress

- Who will work on each goal, and how often?
- Will my child be supported in class, outside class, or both?
- How will progress be measured and recorded?
- How and how often will you share progress with me?

### About home

- What can we practise at home that supports these goals?
- Is there anything we should avoid doing at home that might confuse things?
- Who should I contact if I notice something between meetings?

You do not need to ask every question. Pick the ones that fit your child.

## How do I share what I have observed at home?

Keep it short and specific. Link each observation to a goal where you can. For example: "For the goal on asking for help, I have seen him do this at home three or four times this month, mostly with his grandmother."

If what you see at home is different from what the school sees, say so calmly. Children often behave differently in different places. Neither view is wrong. Both help the team understand your child better.

It also helps to share what is going well. Strengths are useful information. They can shape how a goal is taught.

## What if I disagree with something?

It is normal to have a different view. You can say, "I'm not sure about this goal. Can you help me understand why it was chosen?" Or, "I would like to think about this before agreeing."

You do not have to sign or agree to everything in the room. It is reasonable to ask for time, a follow-up conversation, or a written summary. Most schools want parents on board, because the plan works better that way.

## What should I do after the meeting?

### Write a short summary the same day

While it is fresh, note:

- the goals agreed, in your own words
- who is responsible for what
- what you agreed to practise at home
- when progress will next be shared
- anything that was left open

If the school sends minutes, compare them with your notes. If something is missing, a polite email is enough to clear it up.

### Keep tracking between meetings

The next meeting will be easier if you keep a light record of how each goal is going at home. A few lines a week is plenty. Our guide on [how to track IEP goals at home](/blog/track-iep-goals-at-home) explains a simple way to do this.

If your child also sees therapists outside school, try to keep one shared record so everyone works from the same picture. See [keeping one record for many therapists](/blog/one-record-for-many-therapists).

## A short checklist

- Get the current plan and reports early.
- Write dated, specific notes from home.
- Pick two or three priorities.
- Bring your questions and a way to take notes.
- Ask for time if you need it.
- Write a summary afterwards and keep tracking.

## In closing

You are an important member of your child's team. The school knows your child in the classroom. You know your child everywhere else. A good IEP meeting brings those views together.
