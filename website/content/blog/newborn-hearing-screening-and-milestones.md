Hearing loss in babies is not something you can see. A baby who cannot hear well may still smile, coo and look perfectly content. That is why many hospitals offer a quick hearing test before a newborn goes home, and why it helps to know which listening milestones to notice in the first year.

This guide explains the tests, what the results mean and what to do if you are worried. It is general information, not a substitute for advice from your child's doctor or audiologist.

## Why test hearing at birth

Babies start learning language from the sounds around them long before they say a word. When hearing loss is found early, families can get support while this learning is happening. The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/deafness-and-hearing-loss) lists newborns and infants among the groups who should be screened, and calls early identification key to effective care. Waiting until a child is not talking can mean losing months that matter.

## The 1-3-6 guideline in plain words

Many newborn hearing programmes aim for a simple set of goals called 1-3-6, set out by the [Joint Committee on Infant Hearing](https://www.jcih.org/JCIH_2019_Executive_Summary.pdf) and widely used as a benchmark:

- **By 1 month:** the baby has a hearing screening.
- **By 3 months:** if the screening is not passed, a full hearing assessment confirms whether there is hearing loss.
- **By 6 months:** a baby with hearing loss has started early intervention, such as hearing aids and support for the family.

These are targets, not deadlines. If your baby missed a step, it is still worth doing it now.

## The two screening tests

Both tests are quick, painless and usually done while the baby sleeps.

- **OAE (otoacoustic emissions):** a small soft probe in the ear plays quiet sounds and measures the echo from the inner ear.
- **Automated ABR (auditory brainstem response):** small sticky sensors on the head measure how the hearing nerve responds to sounds.

In India, paediatric guidance recommends two stages: babies are screened first with OAE, and those who do not pass go on to automated ABR. Before you leave hospital, ask when and where any repeat test will be done.

## What "pass" and "refer" mean

A **pass** means the test found a typical response at that moment.

A **refer** result means the baby needs another test. It is not a diagnosis. Fluid in the ear after birth, a restless baby or background noise can all cause a refer. What matters is going back for the repeat test and, if needed, the full assessment by an audiologist.

## When to keep a closer watch

Some babies need hearing checked again later, even if they passed at birth. Ask your doctor whether your baby needs follow-up if, for example, there was a long stay in the newborn intensive care unit, a family history of childhood hearing loss, jaundice severe enough to need an exchange transfusion, or certain infections before or after birth. Your doctor can tell you what applies to your baby.

## Listening milestones to notice

Every child develops at their own pace, so treat these as rough guides rather than a test.

- **Birth to 3 months:** reacts to loud sounds; calms down or smiles when spoken to, and recognises your voice.
- **4 to 6 months:** follows sounds with their eyes; notices toys that make sounds.
- **7 to 12 months:** turns and looks towards sounds; babbles with long and short groups of sounds; understands words for common things like "cup" or "shoe".
- **Around 12 months:** may say one or two words and respond to simple requests like "come here".

These are adapted from the [NIDCD hearing and communication checklist](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/your-babys-hearing-and-communicative-development-checklist).

If your child seems to miss sounds, stops babbling, or does not respond to their name, ask for a hearing test. A hearing test can be done at any age, and an audiologist is the right person to do it.

## RBSK and follow-up in India

Under [Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK)](https://nhm.gov.in/index1.php?lang=1&level=4&sublinkid=1190&lid=583), children are screened for a set of conditions that include congenital deafness, and young children who need follow-up are referred to district-level services such as District Early Intervention Centres (DEIC). How screening is done varies between hospitals and states, so before you leave hospital, ask whether your baby's hearing test has been done and where to go for a repeat if needed.

## Common questions

### Does a refer result mean my baby cannot hear?

No. A refer result means the baby needs another test. Many babies who are referred pass the repeat test. Go back for it as soon as you are asked.

### Is the newborn hearing test painful?

No. Both OAE and automated ABR are painless and are usually done while the baby is asleep.

### My baby passed at birth. Can hearing loss still happen later?

Yes, some hearing loss appears later in childhood. If you notice your child missing sounds or not responding to their name, ask for a hearing test at any age.

### What is the 1-3-6 guideline?

It is a set of goals: screening by 1 month, a full assessment by 3 months if needed, and early intervention by 6 months for babies with hearing loss.
