It often starts at the park, or at a family gathering. Another child the same age is chatting away, or running, or using a spoon on their own. Someone says, "Is yours doing that yet?" And a small worry settles in.

Comparing is very human. Almost every parent does it. But it rarely tells you what you actually want to know: is my child growing and learning, and do they need any extra support?

This article explains what developmental milestones are, what they are not, and a calmer way to think about your child's progress.

## What are developmental milestones?

Developmental milestones are skills that many children learn around a certain stage of life. They are usually grouped into areas like:

- **Movement**, such as sitting, walking, climbing and using hands
- **Communication**, such as understanding words, babbling, talking and using gestures
- **Social and emotional skills**, such as smiling, playing with others and showing feelings
- **Thinking and play**, such as exploring objects, pretend play and solving simple problems
- **Self-care**, such as feeding, dressing and toileting

Doctors and health workers use milestone checklists to see how a child is developing over time. They help spot when a child might benefit from a closer look or some support.

We have not listed specific ages here on purpose. Checklists vary, and the details matter. Your child's paediatrician can show you the checklist they use and talk you through it.

## What milestones are not

Milestones are useful, but they are often misunderstood. It helps to be clear about what they are not.

### They are not a deadline

Milestones usually describe a range, not a single date. Children reach them at different times, and that range can be quite wide.

### They are not a race

A child who walks early is not "ahead" in life. A child who talks later is not "behind" as a person. Each child has their own pace and pattern.

### They are not a full picture

A checklist captures a few skills at one moment. It does not capture your child's personality, interests, humour, kindness or the many small steps that happen in between.

### They are not a diagnosis

Missing a milestone on a checklist does not, on its own, mean anything is wrong. It is a reason to have a conversation with a professional, not a conclusion.

## Why comparing with other children can mislead

When you compare your child with a cousin or a classmate, you are comparing two very different stories. Children differ in:

- temperament and interests
- the languages spoken at home (many children in India and the UAE grow up with two or more)
- their health history
- how much practice they have had with a particular skill
- whether they are a first child or have older siblings to copy

Comparisons also tend to focus on what one child is not doing yet. They rarely notice what your child does well.

And comparing can quietly change how we feel and how we act with our child. Worry can make playtime feel like testing time. Children often sense that.

## A more useful question: how is my child doing compared with before?

Instead of "Is my child doing what other children do?", try asking "What can my child do now that they could not do a few months ago?"

This is what professionals often mean by a child's *baseline*: where your child started. Progress is measured from there.

Looking at your child's own baseline helps because:

- it shows real change, even when it is small
- it fits your child's own pace and path
- it gives you something concrete to share with professionals
- it often brings relief, because you notice steps you had missed

### How to keep track of your child's own progress

You do not need anything complicated. A few habits help:

1. **Write short, dated notes.** "Pointed to the bird and looked at me" is more useful than "communication is better."
2. **Note the context.** Where were you? Who was there? Was your child tired or rested?
3. **Look back every few weeks.** Read your older notes. Many parents are surprised by how much has changed.
4. **Notice new skills and also steadier ones.** Doing something more often or more easily is progress too.
5. **Keep photos or short videos** if your family is comfortable with that. They help you remember.

If your child is in therapy, these notes also help their therapist see what is happening outside sessions. Our guide on [what to write after a therapy session](/blog/what-to-write-after-a-therapy-session) has a simple format.

## When should I talk to a professional?

Comparing less does not mean ignoring your instincts. You know your child well. If something worries you, it is always fine to ask.

Consider talking to your child's paediatrician if:

- your child seems to have lost a skill they used to have
- you have a steady feeling that something is not quite right
- your child is not doing several things on the checklist your doctor uses
- a teacher, caregiver or relative raises a concern you also share
- you simply want reassurance

Bring your notes. Specific examples help the doctor understand what you are seeing. They may suggest waiting and watching, a closer assessment, or a referral to a specialist such as a speech or occupational therapist.

Asking early is never a mistake. If all is well, you get peace of mind. If your child would benefit from support, starting sooner is usually easier for everyone.

## What if my child is already in therapy?

If your child already sees a therapist, their goals are often the clearest guide to progress. Goals are set for your child, based on where they are now.

Ask the therapist:

- What does progress on this goal look like?
- What small changes should I look out for at home?
- How will we know when it is time to move on to the next goal?

Then watch for those small changes. They count, even when they seem tiny next to what other children are doing. Our article on [signs speech therapy is working](/blog/signs-speech-therapy-is-working) has more on what to look for.

## Being kind to yourself

If you have been comparing, you have not done anything wrong. It comes from caring. Many parents find it helps to:

- step back from conversations that leave them anxious
- share their child's wins, however small, with people who will celebrate them
- remember that a checklist describes skills, not your child's worth

## In closing

Milestones are a helpful tool for doctors and parents. They are not a scoreboard. The most useful comparison is between your child today and your child a few months ago.
