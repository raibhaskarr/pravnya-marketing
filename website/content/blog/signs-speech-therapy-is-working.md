After weeks or months of sessions, many parents quietly wonder: is speech therapy working? It is a fair question. Progress in communication is often gradual, and when you see your child every day, small changes are easy to miss.

This article looks at the kinds of signs of progress in speech therapy that parents often notice at home, how to keep track of them, and how to talk openly with your child's therapist.

## Why progress can be hard to see

Communication grows in small steps. A child may first understand a word, then try it with help, then use it on their own, and later use it in new places and with new people. Each step matters, but none of them feels dramatic.

There are other reasons progress can feel slow:

- You see your child every day, so gradual change blends in.
- Progress is rarely a straight line. Holidays, illness, a new school or a change in routine can bring a few harder weeks.
- Some goals work on foundations, such as attention, turn-taking or understanding, before speech itself changes.
- Children often show a new skill in the therapy room before they use it at home.

None of this means therapy isn't helping. It means that looking back over time, rather than day to day, gives a truer picture.

## Signs of progress in speech therapy

Every child is different, and the right signs to look for depend on your child's own goals. These are common kinds of change parents describe. Your child may show some, none or entirely different ones.

### Understanding more

- Following an instruction they used to miss, like "get your shoes and come here".
- Responding to their name more often.
- Understanding a question without a gesture to help.

### Communicating more, in any form

- Trying to communicate more often, with words, signs, pictures, gestures or a device.
- Starting interactions instead of only responding.
- Asking for things, or for help, in a new way.

Communication is much more than speech. A child who begins pointing to share something, or uses a picture card to ask for a snack, is communicating.

### Clearer or longer speech

- Sounds that were tricky becoming clearer in some words.
- Putting two words together where there was one, or longer sentences than before.
- Family members or teachers understanding your child more easily.

### Needing less help

This is one of the most useful signs, and one of the easiest to miss. Perhaps your child once needed you to say a whole word first, and now only needs the first sound. Or they used to need a reminder and now do it on their own. The skill hasn't changed, but your child's independence with it has.

### Using skills in new places

- Using a word at home that they practised in therapy.
- Talking with grandparents, cousins or a shopkeeper.
- Trying a skill at school or the park, not just in the therapy room.

### Confidence and enjoyment

Some changes are less about words and more about ease. A child who used to get frustrated might now try again, or seem to enjoy conversation more. These moments count.

## How to notice speech therapy progress at home

Memory is not a reliable record, especially when you are busy. A few short notes make a big difference.

1. **Ask the therapist which goals they are working on.** Get them in plain words, and ask what progress might look like at home.
2. **Write down what you see.** A line or two, two or three times a week: what your child said or did, where, and how much help they needed.
3. **Keep a few "before" examples.** Note how your child communicates now, or record a short video for yourself. In a few months you'll have something real to compare with.
4. **Read back once a month.** Look for changes in help level, new words or ways of communicating, and new settings.

Compare your child only with their own earlier self. Other children's timelines tell you very little about your child's path.

For ideas on what to note after each session, see [what to write after a therapy session](/blog/what-to-write-after-a-therapy-session).

## How to talk to the speech therapist about progress

Many parents feel awkward asking "Is this working?" Therapists generally welcome the question. It helps them understand what you are seeing and what matters to your family.

Some ways to start the conversation:

- "What changes have you noticed since we started?"
- "What does progress on this goal look like at home?"
- "How much help does my child need now, compared with when we began?"
- "Here's what I've noticed at home. Does that match what you see?"
- "When will we next review the goals together?"

Bring your notes. Real examples from home, with dates, are far more useful than a general feeling. They also help the therapist see whether skills are carrying over beyond sessions.

### If you're worried progress has stalled

It is normal to have periods where change is slow. If you're concerned, say so plainly and kindly. You might ask:

- Whether the goals still fit where your child is now.
- Whether anything at home could support the work, and how.
- Whether it would help to look at other areas, such as hearing, attention or sleep, with your paediatrician.
- How progress will be measured over the next few months.

You are part of your child's team. Asking questions is not a criticism of the therapist; it is part of working together.

## Keeping home practice helpful

Home practice can support therapy, but only when it suits your child and has been agreed with the therapist. A few general principles:

- Ask the therapist exactly what to practise and how often.
- Keep it short and woven into daily life, like naming things at bath time or waiting for your child to ask during snacks.
- Stop if your child is upset or tired. Pressure tends to make communication harder, not easier.
- Celebrate the attempt, not just the result.

## In short

- Progress in speech therapy is often gradual, and easy to miss day to day.
- Look for understanding, communicating more in any form, clearer or longer speech, less help needed, skills in new places, and more confidence.
- Keep short, dated notes and read them back monthly.
- Talk openly with your child's therapist, bringing real examples from home.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your child's therapist knows your child best. If you have concerns about your child's communication or progress, please raise them with your therapist or paediatrician.*
